# WP-AF-0008 — Define the Monad Knowledge Lifecycle

## Executive Summary

Create the canonical architecture-level description of how engineering knowledge enters, moves through, persists within, leaves, and feeds back into the Monad ecosystem.

The resulting `vision/knowledge-lifecycle.md` will define the complete lifecycle from initial observation or intent through:

* capture;
* representation;
* identification;
* compilation;
* normalization;
* semantic analysis;
* MSG construction;
* validation;
* persistence in MKE;
* versioning;
* query;
* projection;
* application;
* observation;
* evidence capture;
* revision;
* adoption;
* supersession;
* withdrawal;
* archival.

The lifecycle must explain not only the happy path but also how Monad preserves:

* uncertainty;
* incomplete knowledge;
* conflicts;
* rejected claims;
* historical versions;
* authority transitions;
* lifecycle transitions;
* provenance;
* derivation;
* evidence;
* feedback.

This document will connect the compiler pipeline to the persistent Knowledge Engine and all downstream projections while preserving the distinction between knowledge, representations, and operations.

---

# Problem Statement

The Architecture Map and Compiler Pipeline explain the structure of Monad and how source artifacts become a compiled semantic graph.

They do not yet provide a complete account of what happens to knowledge across its entire lifetime.

Important questions remain distributed across several documents:

* When does information become knowledge?
* What is canonical before and after compilation?
* How is knowledge persisted without losing its compilation lineage?
* How does a new graph snapshot relate to earlier snapshots?
* How do observations, tests, runtime evidence, and human review feed back into the system?
* How does inferred or generated knowledge gain authority?
* How are errors corrected without rewriting history?
* What happens when knowledge is deprecated, superseded, withdrawn, or archived?
* How do publications and generated code relate back to their semantic sources?
* How are manual changes to derived outputs handled?
* How is knowledge deleted, redacted, expired, or legally removed while preserving auditability?
* How does Monad distinguish correction, revision, migration, supersession, and contradiction?

Without a canonical lifecycle model, the compiler, Knowledge Engine, publication systems, AI services, and engineering workflow could implement incompatible assumptions about how knowledge changes.

---

# Motivation

Monad's central architectural claim is that engineering knowledge is the primary artifact.

That claim requires a precise lifecycle.

A knowledge-first system must be able to explain:

* where knowledge came from;
* which representation captured it;
* which compilation produced it;
* which graph snapshot contains it;
* which persistent versions preserve it;
* what authority it possesses;
* where it applies;
* what derives from it;
* what changed it;
* what replaced it;
* what evidence supports or challenges it.

The Knowledge Lifecycle will establish the conceptual spine connecting:

```text
Reality and Intent
        ↓
Source Artifacts
        ↓
MSC
        ↓
MSG
        ↓
MKE
        ↓
Projections and Application
        ↓
New Evidence and Decisions
        ↓
New Source Artifacts and Compilation
```

# Authority Evolution

Authority does not increase automatically through processing.

```text
Captured information
        │
        ├── observed
        ├── imported
        ├── inferred
        └── generated
        │
        ▼
Validation and review
        │
        ▼
Candidate or provisional knowledge
        │
        ▼
Authorized adoption
        │
        ▼
Normative or approved knowledge
```

## Authority Rules

1. Parsing does not increase authority.
2. Compilation does not increase authority.
3. Persistence does not increase authority.
4. Publication does not increase authority.
5. AI generation does not increase authority.
6. Repetition does not increase authority.
7. Adoption requires an authorized actor or process.
8. Authority may be context-specific.
9. Authority may be reduced by import mode.
10. Authority changes must preserve their basis.
11. Conflicting authority claims must remain visible.
12. Withdrawal does not erase history.

# Revision Model

The deliverable must distinguish:

## Editorial Revision

Changes representation without intended semantic change.

Examples:

* spelling;
* formatting;
* clarified wording;
* navigation.

## Representation Migration

Moves the same intended knowledge to another format or schema.

Examples:

* Markdown to canonical MSL;
* YAML schema version change;
* storage migration.

## Semantic Revision

Changes the meaning of a claim or artifact.

Requires:

* new version;
* change rationale;
* affected relationships;
* downstream invalidation.

## Correction

Repairs knowledge known to be wrong.

A correction preserves:

* incorrect prior version;
* correction rationale;
* supporting evidence;
* effective point;
* affected projections.

## Extension

Adds meaning without invalidating prior meaning.

Compatibility must be assessed.

## Retraction

States that a prior claim should no longer be relied upon because it was unsupported, incorrect, or improperly issued.

## Supersession

Replaces a prior artifact or claim for future applicability.

## Historical Annotation

Adds context to historical knowledge without changing what was asserted at the time.

# Snapshot and History Model

MSG snapshots are immutable semantic products of compilation.

MKE preserves them through explicit relationships.

Conceptually:

```text
MSG Snapshot A
      │
      ├── revised_by ──► MSG Snapshot B
      │
      ├── branched_to ─► MSG Snapshot C
      │
      └── superseded_by ► MSG Snapshot D
```

## Required History Concepts

* parent snapshot;
* child snapshot;
* branch;
* merge;
* common ancestor;
* semantic diff;
* conflict set;
* effective view;
* historical view;
* supersession boundary;
* migration relationship.

## History Rules

1. A new snapshot does not mutate its parent.
2. A current effective view is a query result, not erasure of history.
3. Merges preserve source lineages.
4. Conflicts remain explicit.
5. Semantic diffs operate on identity and meaning, not text alone.
6. Storage compaction must preserve required history semantics.
7. Historical queries must identify the requested temporal or version context.

# Evidence Lifecycle

Evidence has its own lifecycle.

```text
Observed
   ↓
Captured
   ↓
Identified
   ↓
Validated
   ↓
Linked to Claim
   ↓
Used in Evaluation
   ↓
Revalidated, Expired, Invalidated, Withdrawn, or Superseded
```

## Evidence Rules

1. Evidence must have identity.
2. Evidence must preserve origin.
3. Evidence must identify applicable context.
4. Evidence may support or contradict several claims.
5. Evidence validity may expire.
6. Evidence invalidation must invalidate dependent conclusions where required.
7. Test results are evidence, not eternal facts.
8. Runtime observations require environment context.
9. Manual approval is evidence of approval, not proof of technical correctness.
10. AI output may be evidence of a suggestion, not evidence that the suggestion is true.

# Projection Lifecycle

A projection is derived from canonical knowledge for a purpose.

```text
Canonical Knowledge
        ↓
Projection Request
        ↓
Selection and Transformation
        ↓
Rendered Representation
        ↓
Publication or Application
        ↓
Expiration, Regeneration, or Withdrawal
```

## Projection Metadata

A projection should preserve:

* projection identity;
* semantic source snapshot;
* query or selection;
* transformation;
* renderer;
* renderer version;
* audience;
* target format;
* generated time or version;
* omissions;
* losses;
* authority;
* expiration policy.

## Manual Editing

A manually edited generated projection must be treated as one of:

* prohibited modification;
* noncanonical local overlay;
* new source artifact requiring re-ingestion;
* patch against a declared projection model.

It must not silently become canonical knowledge.

## Regeneration

Regeneration should either:

* replace the prior projection while preserving history; or
* create a new version.

2. AI output may not silently alter canonical knowledge.

5. Adoption requires an authorized process.
6. Regenerated output is a new artifact or version.
7. Model changes may invalidate reproducibility.

# Conflict and Uncertainty Lifecycle

Monad preserves several nonfinal states.

## Unknown

Insufficient information exists.

May become resolved through new evidence or declarations.

## Deferred

A known dependency prevents present conclusion.

Carries a resume condition.

## Ambiguous

Several valid interpretations remain.

May be resolved by clarification, stronger identity, profile, or governance.

## Conflicting

Claims cannot simultaneously hold under the active context.

Requires preservation, separation, or governed resolution.

## Contested

Authority or acceptance is disputed.

May continue indefinitely while both claims remain represented.

## Invalid

Applicable rules are violated.

May be corrected through revision.

## Waived

A known violation has been explicitly permitted within a limited context.

A waiver does not erase the violation.

# Retention, Redaction, and Deletion

The lifecycle document must establish architecture-level handling of removal.

## Retention

Defines how long knowledge or evidence must remain available.

## Archival

Moves inactive knowledge into preserved historical status.

## Restricted Access

Limits visibility without changing semantic existence.

## Redaction

Removes or obscures sensitive representation while retaining a record that redaction occurred.

## Tombstone

Preserves identity and removal metadata after content is unavailable.

## Cryptographic Erasure

Makes protected content inaccessible by destroying required encryption material while preserving permitted metadata.

## Legal Deletion

Removes content required to be deleted by law or policy.

Where permitted, the system should preserve:

* tombstone identity;
* deletion authority;
* reason category;
* effective time;
* affected relationships.

## Deletion Rules

1. Deletion must not be simulated by ordinary supersession.
2. Access restriction is not deletion.
3. Redaction is not semantic correction.
4. Tombstones must not leak removed sensitive content.
5. Derived projections containing deleted content must be invalidated.
6. Caches and indexes must follow deletion policy.
7. AI context derived from deleted content must be invalidated where required.
8. Legal and privacy policy may override ordinary historical-preservation principles.

# Lifecycle Invariants

The completed document must preserve at least these invariants.

1. Knowledge transitions are explicit.
2. Every persistent semantic item has identity.
3. Every transformation preserves provenance.
4. Compilation does not automatically increase authority.
5. Persistence does not automatically increase authority.
6. Publication does not automatically increase authority.
7. AI generation does not automatically increase authority.
8. Authority and lifecycle remain independent axes.
9. Semantic revision creates a new version or snapshot.
10. Historical knowledge is not silently overwritten.
11. Corrections preserve the corrected prior state.
12. Supersession preserves predecessor identity.
13. Withdrawal does not imply deletion.
14. Archival does not imply deletion.
15. Redaction does not imply semantic correction.
16. Deletion invalidates affected projections and caches.
17. Evidence changes invalidate dependent conclusions where required.
18. Projections remain traceable to canonical knowledge.
19. Manual edits to projections do not silently alter canonical knowledge.
20. Runtime outcomes become knowledge only through explicit capture.
21. AI outputs remain distinguishable from human-authored and validated knowledge.
22. Unknown, deferred, ambiguous, contested, and conflicting states remain explicit.
23. MKE history is versioned and query-contextual.
24. MSG snapshots are immutable.
25. Current effective knowledge is a view over history.
26. Representation migration is distinct from semantic revision.
27. Lifecycle events identify actors, causes, and state transitions.
28. Legal deletion policy may require controlled exceptions to ordinary historical preservation.
29. Every feedback loop re-enters through a governed boundary.
30. Self-hosting must preserve the same lifecycle rules applied to external projects.

# Bootstrap Success Scenario

A minimal lifecycle scenario should support:

```text
1. Author creates specification artifact.
2. Artifact receives stable identity.
3. MSC compiles artifact into MSG.
4. MSG records source and compilation provenance.
5. MSG is exported or persisted.
6. Author revises the specification.
7. MSC creates a new MSG snapshot.
8. New snapshot links to the prior snapshot.
9. Semantic diff identifies changed claims.
10. One claim is marked superseded.
11. Documentation projection is regenerated from the new snapshot.
12. Historical projection remains traceable to the old snapshot.
```

This scenario demonstrates identity, provenance, immutability, versioning, supersession, and projection regeneration.

# Self-Hosting Closure

Monad's knowledge lifecycle eventually applies to Monad itself.

```text
Monad engineering intent
        ↓

        ↓
MSC compilation
        ↓
Monad project MSG
        ↓
MKE persistence
        ↓
Documentation, status, code, and AI context projections
        ↓
Implementation and operation
        ↓
Tests, commits, observations, and outcomes
        ↓
Evidence and journal artifacts
        ↓
New work packets and specification revisions
        ↓
MSC compilation again
```

Self-hosting is complete only when:

* Monad artifacts follow the same identity rules;
* changes follow the same lifecycle rules;
* projections remain derived;
* work outcomes become evidence;
* AI assistance remains governed;
* the project can explain why its current state exists.

# Architectural Impact

## Classification

**Architecture stabilization and lifecycle integration.**

This work packet formalizes how accepted Monad concepts evolve through time.

It should not introduce an unrelated subsystem.

## Permitted Findings

The work may identify:

* overloaded state vocabularies;
* unclear authority transitions;
* missing history relationships;
* inadequate deletion semantics;
* projection feedback ambiguity;
* inconsistent evidence handling;
* incomplete MKE boundaries.

Such findings must be handled through:

* glossary clarification;
* lifecycle-document clarification;
* future MGO, MSG, or MKE specifications;
* an ADR when a significant architectural change is required.

## Prohibited Actions

This work packet must not:

* treat persistence as compilation;
* treat publication as authority;
* treat AI generation as adoption;
* overwrite historical snapshots;
* conflate lifecycle with authority;
* conflate semantic revision with representation migration;
* treat supersession as deletion;
* allow generated-output edits to silently alter canonical knowledge;
* imply that all knowledge must be retained forever;
* define one database's event model as the architecture;
* require event sourcing without an ADR;
* claim full legal compliance without jurisdiction-specific policy.

# Risks

## Risk 1 — Overloaded Lifecycle

One linear lifecycle may be applied incorrectly to artifacts, claims, evidence, and projections.

**Mitigation**

* Define separate lifecycle domains and state axes.

## Risk 2 — Historical Immutability Absolutism

The architecture may conflict with privacy, security, or legal deletion needs.

**Mitigation**

* Define controlled redaction, tombstone, and deletion concepts.

## Risk 3 — Authority Inflation

Compilation, validation, persistence, or publication may be treated as authority promotion.

**Mitigation**

* Require explicit adoption transitions.

## Risk 4 — Event-Sourcing Assumption

Conceptual lifecycle events may be mistaken for a mandatory storage architecture.

**Mitigation**

* State that events are semantic concepts, not an implementation mandate.

## Risk 5 — Feedback Bypass

Runtime observations or AI outputs may mutate knowledge without compilation or governance.

**Mitigation**

* Require explicit evidence capture and re-entry.

## Risk 6 — Snapshot Explosion

Immutable history may create unbounded storage expectations.

**Mitigation**

* Separate semantic history requirements from storage-retention implementation.

## Risk 7 — Projection Drift

Generated outputs may be edited independently and diverge from knowledge.

**Mitigation**

* Define overlays and explicit re-ingestion.
