# 1. Purpose

This assessment identifies the student’s readiness for geographical learning.

At the Kindergarten–Grade 1 foundation level, geography begins with direct experience of place and space.

It includes the student’s ability to:

* describe where objects are;
* follow and give simple directions;
* recognize familiar routes;
* understand that maps represent real places;
* interpret basic map symbols;
* create simple maps;
* identify landmarks;
* compare nearby and distant places;
* recognize common landforms and bodies of water;
* understand that weather and environment influence how people live;
* identify basic connections among home, community, state, country, continent, and planet;
* observe how people use and change places;
* ask geographical questions.

The central question is:

> How does this student understand location, place, movement, environment, and spatial relationships?

The results should guide map work, local exploration, field studies, nature study, community learning, and the student’s Personal Learning Plan.

---

# 2. Scope and Limitations

This artifact is an educational diagnostic.

It is not designed to:

* assign a grade;
* diagnose visual-spatial or developmental conditions;
* require memorization of states, capitals, countries, or continents;
* test mastery of a particular geography curriculum;
* compare the student with other children.

A child may demonstrate strong geographical reasoning without knowing formal terms such as *cardinal direction*, *landform*, *scale*, or *region*.

For example, a student who says:

> “The library is past the store and across from the park.”

is demonstrating useful spatial reasoning even without formal map vocabulary.

# 3. Assessment Principles

## 3.1 Begin with familiar places

Geographical understanding develops outward:

1. body and personal space;
2. room;
3. home;
4. yard;
5. neighborhood;
6. community;
7. state;
8. country;
9. continent;
10. world.

The assessment begins with direct experience before moving toward abstract representations.

## 3.2 Accept multiple forms of response

The student may demonstrate understanding by:

* pointing;
* moving an object;
* following a route;
* drawing;
* describing;
* building a model;
* using gestures;
* labeling.

Written responses are not required for every task.

## 3.3 Distinguish map skill from drawing skill

A student may understand where things belong while lacking fine-motor control or drawing detail.

Evaluate spatial organization separately from artistic quality.

## 3.4 Ask for reasoning

Use neutral prompts:

* “How do you know?”
* “What is near it?”
* “What would you pass on the way?”
* “How could someone find it?”
* “What symbol could represent that?”
* “What might be different in another place?”

## 3.5 Use real places whenever possible

Local walks, familiar rooms, photographs, maps, and landmarks produce stronger evidence than abstract worksheets alone.

# 5. Developmental Rating Scale

Use this scale in the final profile.

## Level 0 — Not Yet Observed

The student did not demonstrate the competency or the task was not sufficiently completed.

## Level 1 — Beginning

The student recognizes familiar places and basic position words with substantial support.

## Level 2 — Emerging

The student follows simple spatial directions, identifies familiar landmarks, and interprets basic picture maps with prompting.

## Level 3 — Developing

The student independently uses position language, follows familiar routes, creates simple maps, and connects symbols with real places.

## Level 4 — Proficient

The student interprets and creates maps, uses directions and landmarks, compares places, and explains relationships among people and environments.

## Level 5 — Extending

The student reasons about scale, route efficiency, environmental influence, regions, multiple representations, and unfamiliar geographical problems.

# 8. Assessment 1 — Position and Location Words

**Competency:** Understand and use common spatial-position vocabulary.

**Suggested time:** 5–10 minutes

**Materials:** Toy figure, chair, table, two books

## Purpose

To determine whether the student understands words used to describe where objects are located.

## Administration script

Place the toy figure in various locations.

Ask the student to place it:

* on the chair;
* under the chair;
* beside the chair;
* behind the chair;
* in front of the chair;
* between two books;
* inside a container;
* outside the container;
* above a book;
* below a tabletop;
* near the parent-teacher;
* far from the parent-teacher.

Then reverse roles.

Place the object and ask:

> “Tell me where the figure is.”

# 9. Assessment 2 — Following a Route

**Competency:** Follow a sequence of movement directions through a familiar space.

**Suggested time:** 5–10 minutes

**Materials:** Familiar room or home area

## Administration script

Begin with two-step directions.

Example:

> “Walk to the door, then stand beside the table.”

Progress gradually.

### Level A — Two steps

* Walk to the window.
* Turn around.
* Stand beside the chair.

### Level B — Three steps

* Walk to the doorway.
* Turn left.
* Stop beside the bookshelf.

### Level C — Route with landmarks

> “Start at the table. Walk past the couch, turn toward the hallway, and stop at the bedroom door.”

Do not repeat the full direction unless necessary. Record whether repetition was required.

# 11. Assessment 4 — Map of a Familiar Room

**Competency:** Represent a real place using a simple overhead map.

**Suggested time:** 10–15 minutes

**Materials:** Blank paper, pencil, crayons, ruler

## Purpose

To determine whether the student understands that a map can represent the arrangement of objects in a real space.

## Administration script

Stand in a familiar room.

Say:

> “Imagine we could look down through the ceiling. Draw a map showing where the main things in this room are.”

Do not draw an example before the student attempts the task.

If the student is unsure, clarify:

> “Your map can show the walls, door, windows, and large furniture. It does not need to look like a picture.”

# 13. Assessment 5 — Map Symbols and Legend

**Competency:** Interpret and create simple symbols representing real features.

**Suggested time:** 10 minutes

**Materials:**

* simple map containing four to six symbols;
* separate map legend;
* blank paper;
* pencil and crayons.

## Purpose

To determine whether the student understands that a map may use simplified marks, shapes, or pictures to represent real objects and places.

## Preparation

Create or print a simple imaginary park map containing:

* a blue line representing a stream;
* a green circle representing a tree;
* a black rectangle representing a building;
* a red cross representing a first-aid station;
* a dotted line representing a walking path;
* a star representing a meeting place.

Include a legend matching each symbol to its meaning.

## Administration script

Place the map and legend in front of the student.

Say:

> “This map uses symbols to show places and things. The box beside it explains what each symbol means.”

Ask:

1. What does the blue line represent?
2. Which symbol shows a tree?
3. Where is the building?
4. What does the dotted line show?
5. Which symbol marks the meeting place?
6. How could someone use the legend when they do not recognize a symbol?

Do not identify the symbols before recording the student’s first response.

# 14. Assessment 6 — Landmarks and Familiar Routes

**Competency:** Use landmarks to describe, remember, and navigate familiar routes.

**Suggested time:** 10–15 minutes

**Materials:**

* familiar route, such as home to mailbox, bedroom to kitchen, or home to library;
* optional photographs of landmarks;
* blank paper.

## Purpose

To determine whether the student notices stable features that help identify locations and routes.

## Administration option A — Direct route

Walk a safe and familiar route with the student.

Ask:

* What do we pass first?
* What tells you we are going the correct way?
* Which place or object is easiest to recognize?
* What do we pass just before reaching our destination?
* How would you explain this route to someone else?

## Administration option B — Verbal reconstruction

When a walk is impractical, ask the student to describe a familiar route from memory.

Examples:

* bedroom to kitchen;
* front door to mailbox;
* home to a frequently visited store;
* library entrance to the children’s section.

# 15. Assessment 7 — Reading a Simple Map

**Competency:** Locate features and follow a route on a basic map.

**Suggested time:** 10 minutes

**Materials:**

A simple imaginary town map containing:

* four roads;
* a house;
* a park;
* a library;
* a store;
* a school;
* a bridge;
* a river;
* a compass rose or north arrow;
* a legend.

## Purpose

To determine whether the student can extract location and route information from a map rather than merely naming its pictures.

## Administration script

Place the map before the student.

Say:

> “This map shows a small town. Look at it carefully.”

Ask:

1. Point to the library.
2. What is beside the park?
3. What is across the road from the school?
4. Which feature crosses the river?
5. What place is nearest the house?
6. Trace a route from the house to the library.
7. What would you pass along the way?
8. Can you find another route?
9. Which route appears shorter? Why?
10. Which direction is north on this map?

# 16. Assessment 8 — Creating a Route Map

**Suggested time:** 15–20 minutes

**Materials:**

* blank paper;
* pencil;
* crayons;
* ruler, optional.

## Purpose

To determine whether the student can translate remembered movement through real space into a two-dimensional representation.

## Administration script

Ask the student to select one familiar route.

* bedroom to kitchen;
* house to mailbox;

Say:

> “Draw a map that would help another person follow this route.”
